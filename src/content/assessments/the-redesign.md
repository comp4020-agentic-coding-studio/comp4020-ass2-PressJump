## The brief

> Change one thing about your queue. Show your working. Name the loser.

The final piece, and the one the whole semester has been arranged around. You
have measured this queue since week 1. Now change it.

One change. Not a programme of reform, not a new building, not an app. Open a
second counter, merge two lines into one, move the sign, change the discipline
from first-come-first-served to shortest-job-first, put the forms before the
queue instead of after it, add an appointment slot. The constraint is
deliberate. A single change is the only kind you can actually predict, and
predicting it is 35 percent of the mark.

You predict before you are told. Take your week 1 numbers, apply the
arithmetic from weeks 3, 4 and 9, and write down what you expect to happen to
the mean wait, with the working on the page. If you can trial the change,
trial it, and the gap between the prediction and the result is worth more
marks than a prediction that happened to land.

Then the part that is worth 30 percent on its own. Every change to a queue
moves waiting rather than removing it. Somebody waits longer after your
redesign than before it. Find them, say who they are, and put a number on it
if you can. A redesign that claims to help everybody has not been looked at
hard enough, and that is the single most common way this assignment loses
marks.

## What you submit

Two things.

A written proposal of at most 2000 words, including the arithmetic and the
losers. Diagrams are welcome and do not count towards the length.

A defence in the week 12 field session. Eight minutes, then questions from the
room and from whoever runs your site if they agree to come. Several students
each year bring the manager of the shop or the desk they have been measuring.
That is not required and it is the best version of this.

## How the defence is marked

Not on polish. On whether you can hold a position under a question you did not
expect, and change it when the question is a good one. Both of those are
marked well.
