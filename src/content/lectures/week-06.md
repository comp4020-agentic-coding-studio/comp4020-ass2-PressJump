Last week before the break, and the week I care most about.

Week 5 left you with priority as a mechanism and a queasy feeling about who
sets the classes. This week is about the one field that had to face that
question head on, could not avoid it, and produced a public, documented,
argued-over answer. Emergency triage is the most examined queue discipline in
existence and almost nobody outside medicine has read it.

A triage scale is a priority queue where the cost of being in the wrong class
is measured in lives. It is also, unlike almost every other priority system
you will meet this semester, written down, taught, audited and revised. That
combination is rare enough to be worth a lecture on its own.

## What a triage category actually is

We work through a five-category scale. What each category promises, what
evidence it was set from, how the promise is audited, and what happens to the
categories under load.

Then the part that is never in the summary. Triage does not rank people by how
sick they are. It ranks them by how much the waiting will cost them, which is
a different and much stranger quantity, and it is why a person in agony can
correctly be seen after somebody who looks fine.

## Where it breaks

- Under sustained overload, triage stops being a queue discipline and becomes
  rationing, and the scale was not designed for that.
- The categories encode assumptions about who presents and how, and those
  assumptions are not uniform across a population.
- Undertriage and overtriage have very different costs and the same audit.

## The transferable part

You are not going to build a triage scale. What transfers is the demand
triage makes and almost nothing else does. Say what your categories promise,
in units, in public, and then measure whether you kept it.

Hold that against the next queue that quietly sorts you.
