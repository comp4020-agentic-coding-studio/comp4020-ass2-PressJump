Guest lecture, and the hinge of the semester.

Everything up to the break was a queue you could see. You could count it,
stand in it, time it. That property is unusual. Visa applications, social
housing registers, court lists, insurance claims, specialist referrals and
complaints to an ombudsman are all queues, and almost none of them will tell
you your position, the length of the line, or the rate at which it moves.

Marta Ilves spent eleven years processing applications and four more
investigating the offices that process them. She is here to describe what
happens to a queue when nobody outside can measure it, and what happens to the
people inside it who can.

## What the invisibility does

- You cannot balk. Leaving a queue you cannot see the length of is a decision
  made without information, so people stay in queues that will never serve
  them.
- You cannot plan. A wait of unknown length is not a wait, it is an open
  question, and week 8 is about what that costs a person.
- You cannot complain about your position, only about the whole system, and
  systems absorb that easily.
- Last-come-first-served creeps in. Without an audited order, the file on top
  of the pile is served first, and the file on top is the one that was touched
  most recently.

## What it does to the office

The half of this lecture that surprises people. An office whose backlog is
invisible to the public is usually one whose backlog is poorly visible to
itself, and the staff have their own version of the problem.

## Measuring anyway

Three techniques for putting a number on a queue that publishes nothing.
Freedom of information requests, cohort tracking from the outside, and the
timestamp trail a system leaves whether it means to or not.

## After this lecture

You can estimate the length of a queue that refuses to tell you.
